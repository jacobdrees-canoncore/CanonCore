## GAPS — ABSENT FROM CANONCORE (MEDIUM and HIGH only)

Ordered roughly by how early an implementer hits them. LOW-rated items stay in the entries above.

**1. A provider can only return statements and artwork. It cannot return a FILE. — MEDIUM**
Four independent instances in this shard: TV theme music (#116, 2008), Alexandria agents whose
stated remit was "any sort of data, such as **TV theme music, subtitles, and song lyrics**" (#231,
2010), subtitles-on-demand (#193, #212, 2018), and lyrics throughout the music posts. CanonCore's
providers propose statements against a declared field set; artwork has its own table because it
carries a licence, an attribution and a palette; there is no third table. A subtitle or a theme
carries a licence and an attribution exactly as artwork does. The refusals do not cover this —
"no artwork uploads, no artwork scanning" governs *artwork*, and "the scanner takes media files
and playback sidecars, and nothing else" governs *disk*, not the network. So this is genuinely
unspecified, and subtitles-on-demand is the most-requested feature in this whole category.

**2. Search must return entities, and the exclude-by-kind rule reads as if it forbids that. — MEDIUM**
The standing rule says "Entities must not leak into work-browsing surfaces ... work-browsing
surfaces query for `work` and ignore the entity kinds, and entity containers are reached
deliberately." Two Plex posts show what "deliberately" has to mean in practice: search returns
"genres, actors, directors, playlists, as well as all the obvious media titles" (#196), and an
actor is reached by "the **People section of search results**" (#256). Groups scope search, but
nothing says search is exempt from the kind filter. Written as it is, an implementer will apply
the exclusion everywhere and produce a catalogue whose people, characters and places exist and
cannot be found. One sentence fixes it.

**3. Nothing names the relation between a work and its trailer, interview, live version or
lyric video. — MEDIUM-HIGH**
The sharpest gap in the shard, and it arrives from three directions: music videos "associated with
tracks or artists ... regular music videos, **live** music videos, **lyrical** music videos, or even
**interviews**" (#53); Extras as licensed trailers and behind-the-scenes (#246); intro and
end-credit markers (#127). Under the prompt's own rules a lyric video is the same content
differently rendered (an edition), a live performance is different content (a separate item via
`based_on`), and an interview is a separate work with no relation to the song at all — three
different mechanisms, all attaching to the same item, and the prompt names none of them. This is
where "ADAPTATION VERSUS EDITION" stops being decidable, because a trailer is neither: it is not a
rendering of the film and it is not based on it. The line the prompt draws ("Ask what changed: the
content, or the rendering") does not answer it, and it is the first question a real film catalogue
asks.

**4. A DERIVED claim has no legal `source`. — MEDIUM**
`source` "NAMES A PROVIDER OR THE OWNER" and there is no third kind. But three things in this
shard are computed locally rather than asserted by anyone: photo classifier tags (#137), sonic
similarity vectors (#213), and intro/credit markers (#127). CanonCore already has one of these —
the extracted palette — and it dodges the problem by being a column on the artwork row rather
than a statement. The moment anything else is derived, the provenance model has nowhere to put
it, and "where did this come from?" is exactly the question the statements table exists to
answer. Deciding it costs one sentence now and a migration later.

**5. Importing an ordering from a third party has a home in CMPP, and "no import" hides it. — MEDIUM**
Three instances: OPML podcast import (#125, 2018), Soundiiz playlist transfer (#241, 2018), and
"the ability to **import existing lists from other platforms**" as a 2026 flagship (#26). The
prompt's "WHAT NOT TO BUILD" bans "no fork, no export, no import, no cross-instance sharing" — and
that ban is about *CanonCore instances*. Importing a curated ordering from Letterboxd or Trakt is
a provider `browse` result, which the contract already covers and which the prompt explicitly
values ("browse ... returns a container AND its ordering, so browsing a range yields placements for
free"). As written, an implementer reading "no import" will refuse the one import the design is
built for.

**6. There is no UNMERGE, and merge is the one irreversible correction. — MEDIUM**
Plex shipped split alongside merge from the beginning: "Allow **splitting apart** TV Shows and
Movies in the media manager" (#98, 2010), and move-between-parents plus merge for music (#53).
CanonCore specifies merge — "the loser becomes a PERMANENT ALIAS and stays resolvable forever" —
and nothing about splitting an item back into two. Every catalogue in this category eventually
needs an unmerge, and the alias rule is precisely what makes a mis-merge hard to undo.

**7. There is no named UN-MATCH, and no rule for what happens to the statements a match wrote. — MEDIUM**
Plex had "fixes for the 'Match using' and **un-match** functionality" in 2011 (#103), and #208 shows
the cost of not having it: correcting one bad agent's contributions required **deleting and
re-adding an entire library section**. CanonCore has "REJECTIONS ARE REMEMBERED" (a match not yet
applied) but no operation for detaching one already applied, and no statement about whether its
statements are removed, kept, or demoted. The statements table makes the right answer cheap; the
prompt just does not give it.

**8. No BULK operation over a selection. — MEDIUM**
"rich multi-select, full access to edit things like disc and track number, and the ability to
quickly delete whole albums or artists. You can easily **move tracks between albums, albums between
artists, and merge albums and artists**" (#53). Plex's later answer to per-item curation was to
build a whole phone app for it (#243). CanonCore specifies a per-item review queue and a background
applier, and nothing that acts on a selection. Every correction workflow in this category needs
one, and at archive scale (11,285 stories) a per-item-only design is not usable.

**9. Nothing says how a PROVIDER-PROPOSED TITLE reaches the `title` column. — MEDIUM**
"Agents are now allowed to set a title in the update function for movies" (#37) is the moment Plex
first let a provider overwrite a title. In CanonCore `title` is a column, not a statement, so a
provider's proposed title has no provenance row to sit in and no `rank` to lose to. Every other
field has the source order and the favourite to fall back on; title has neither, and two providers
proposing different titles is the ordinary case, not the edge case.

**10. No stored per-file stream metadata, so "say so plainly" can only fire after playback fails. — MEDIUM**
"Greatly enhanced media analysis ... The latter is **critical in order for players to determine what
files they can consume without transcoding**" (#42, 2012); a per-device profile deciding direct play
(#225); "media flagging" badges (#199); a "By Resolution" facet (#43). CanonCore is direct-play only
and promises "When a file will not play, say so plainly", but says nothing about how it knows.
Without codec/profile/level stored at scan time, the plain statement is a post-mortem.

**11. Embedded technical metadata (ID3, EXIF) is neither refused nor permitted. — MEDIUM**
EXIF-derived photo facets (#50), GPS-derived Places (#137), track numbers parsed from tags and
"unknown artist/album" when they are missing (#103), the album-artist tag (#183). The prompt's
refusals name `.nfo`, artwork uploads and artwork scanning; an embedded-tag read is none of those.
Yet a music library with no provider connected has nothing but ID3 to go on, and the prompt
explicitly accepts that "a private instance with no provider has no artwork" — it does not say
whether it also has no titles.

**12. A shared external identifier that DISAGREES is unspecified. — MEDIUM**
"It gets tricky when these sources **contradict themselves**, when for instance a TVDB show and a TMDB
show link to each other, but **their IMDb IDs differ**. It's a brainteaser to figure out whom you
believe" (#127, from a Plex metadata engineer). The prompt defines only the agreeing case:
"agreeing identifiers between providers are evidence they describe the same work." A disagreeing
shared identifier is a *stronger* signal than a missing one, it arrives immediately in a design
that reaches all providers at once, and an implementer will invent a rule for it.

**13. Content fingerprinting is the only matching signal that works on an unlabelled file. — MEDIUM**
Four instances (#29, #53, #97, #213), the last stating the reason plainly: "There is a lot of music
out there without metadata ... Even if the file doesn't have any tags at all, Gracenote's sonic
fingerprinting can work magic." CanonCore's file identity is SHA1 over size plus the first and last
64KB — Plex's own algorithm — which identifies the same *file* and never the same *work*, so
enrichment matching has only titles and external ids to go on. Not needed for the stop condition;
worth knowing that "REJECTIONS ARE REMEMBERED" is therefore the only feedback signal the design has.

**14. The confidence score must be capable of failing, and nothing measures whether it does. — MEDIUM-HIGH**
"we have a **test set of about 60k filenames** for movies and episodes that we run matching against
**every day** and get alerted quickly if our **accuracy level** were to decrease" (#127). The prompt
states the requirement — "THE CONFIDENCE SCORE MUST BE CAPABLE OF FAILING. A scorer that awards
points for conditions that are always true reaches its threshold before comparing anything" — and
gives no way to observe it. A scorer capable of failing that is never measured is
indistinguishable from one that is not. The cheapest possible fix is a fixture of labelled inputs
with an accuracy floor, and the committed archive extract is the obvious substrate.

**15. No scan ordering rule, and no debounce on filesystem events. — MEDIUM**
"**We scan new media first** (so even if you have a giant library, it's quick to pick up the new
stuff), and we download posters and other information **during** the scan, instead of waiting until
the end" (#53). And the other half: "we **wait until the changes stop before kicking off the scan**,
so that we don't scan partial/locked files" (#105). The prompt says "DO NOT ASSUME FILESYSTEM
CHANGE NOTIFICATIONS FIRE ... Support explicit periodic scans", which covers notifications that
never fire and not notifications that fire too early. This one has teeth: file identity is a hash
over the first and last 64KB, so hashing a file that is still being copied **mints a wrong,
permanent identity**. Also missing is the two-speed scan Plex has had since 2011 — "**Turbo
Scanning** (option-click to do regular 'deep' scan)" (#99) — without which either every scan is
slow or a moved-and-modified file is missed.

**16. No transaction-boundary rule, and this is the named way a scanner breaks playback. — MEDIUM**
"the **scanner thread** ... **creates a big-ass database transaction inside which it does tons of
time-consuming things like network scraping**. This means that (a) **no other database operations can
run, like saving where you were in a file for resuming**" (#157, 2008). CanonCore has three
long-running writers that must not serialise against each other: the scanner, background
enrichment, and the wholesale projection rebuild — while progress writes every 10 seconds. Cheap to
design out; expensive to discover.

**17. No backup or move-an-install story. — MEDIUM**
Plex's own Pro Week session on it (#66) links to "Backing Up Plex Media Server Data" and "Move an
Install to Another System". CanonCore's catalogue is the irreplaceable half — the media are
references, and the review queue, remembered rejections and owner statements are the data nobody
can re-derive — and the prompt says nothing about backup. Postgres makes it ordinary, which is
exactly why it will be assumed rather than decided.

**18. Nothing says whether a source that is REMOVED takes its statements with it. — MEDIUM**
Implied by #213 (Plex deleted a feature when a metadata provider changed terms) and #208 (Plex had
to delete a library section to undo one agent's contributions). CanonCore is structurally able to
answer this — statements carry `source`, so removing a provider's rows leaves everything else
standing — but the prompt does not say what disconnecting a provider actually does, and the three
plausible answers (delete its statements, keep them as historical claims, keep them but stop them
winning) are all defensible and produce visibly different catalogues.

**19. A per-field QUALITY signal, which is the one place Plex overrides its own source order. — MEDIUM**
"The simplest example I can give are summaries: we prefer some sources over others, but **the length
of the summary also adds a score and we penalize summaries that end mid-sentence**" (#127).
CanonCore has exactly two mechanisms — a single global source order and a hand-set `rank` — and
"set BY HAND by the owner, never by a rule" forecloses the automatic version deliberately. But note
what Plex is solving: a value that is *defective* winning because its source sits higher. There is
no notion of a defective value in the model, only a preferred one, so the owner can only fix it
after reading a broken synopsis on the page — which at catalogue scale never happens.

**20. No activity surface for background work. — MEDIUM (low end)**
"a cool new '**activity**' view which shows **what your server is working on** (yes, we will be
enhancing it in the future to show more activity, **which many of you have been asking for**)"
(#244); the Plexamp sonic analysis whose progress is watched "in your web app Activity" and which
"can take a number of hours or even days" (#213). CanonCore runs three background processes and
has one surface, the review queue, which answers "what needs my decision" and not "is it still
working". Plex needed both and was asked for the second one repeatedly.
